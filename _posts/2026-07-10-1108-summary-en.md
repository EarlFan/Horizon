## [EU extends voluntary chat scanning.](https://www.patrick-breyer.de/en/eu-parliament-greenlights-chat-control-1-0-breyer-our-children-lose-out/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The European Parliament allowed the Chat Control 1.0-style voluntary scanning regime for private messages to continue until 2028. A rejection motion received 314 votes against the extension versus 276 in favor and 17 abstentions, but it failed because it needed an absolute majority of 361 MEPs. The decision preserves a legal basis for major platforms to scan private communications for CSAM-related material without a warrant or individualized suspicion. It matters for messaging services, cloud and email providers, and users because it keeps alive a controversial model that critics say weakens privacy and creates pressure around encrypted communications. According to the discussion and linked reporting, the measure applies to voluntary scanning by providers and does not by itself mandate universal scanning of all services. The procedural detail is central: a majority of voting MEPs opposed the extension, but the default outcome stood because rejection required an absolute majority of all MEPs, not merely a majority of votes cast.

**Background**: “Chat Control” is a common label used by critics and digital-rights advocates for EU measures involving detection of child sexual abuse material in digital communications. The 1.0-style regime concerns voluntary scanning, meaning providers may scan certain private messages under a legal carve-out rather than being universally required to do so. Client-side scanning is one proposed technical approach in this policy area, where content such as text, images, or videos is checked on a user’s device before or during transmission. Security and privacy groups argue that such scanning can undermine the trust model of end-to-end encrypted communication because message contents are no longer private only to the sender and recipient.

## [Colibrì runs GLM 5.2 on commodity RAM](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A Show HN project called Colibrì demonstrates a CPU-only approach to running the 744B-parameter GLM 5.2 MoE model on a consumer-class machine with roughly 32 GB of RAM. The author converted parts of the model to int4 and streams routed experts from disk, achieving a very slow but working local setup at about 0.05 to 0.1 tokens per second on low-end hardware. The project is significant because it explores how far local LLM inference can be pushed when the model is far larger than available RAM or VRAM. It is not yet practical for interactive use, but it gives developers concrete techniques for memory-conscious inference, quantization, and disk-backed execution of frontier-scale open models. Colibrì keeps the dense portion of GLM 5.2 resident in RAM at int4, while 21,504 routed experts are stored on disk and loaded on demand using per-layer LRU caching, an optional pinned hot-store, and the operating system page cache. The runtime is a single C implementation with no Python, no BLAS, and no GPU dependency, but the author emphasizes that the measurements are limited to a personal 12-core laptop-class setup.

hackernews · vforno · Jul 9, 08:05 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48842459)

**Background**: GLM 5.2 is described by OpenLM as a flagship open model for coding and long-horizon tasks, with a 1M-token context window and an MIT open-source license. A Mixture-of-Experts model contains many expert subnetworks but activates only a subset for each token, which can reduce compute but still leaves a very large total weight footprint. INT4 quantization stores model weights in 4-bit form, reducing memory and storage requirements at the cost of possible accuracy or implementation trade-offs. Multi-token prediction is a technique in which a model predicts multiple future tokens at once, and related work presents it as a way to improve efficiency or enable faster decoding.

The argument matters beyond the military because it mirrors a broader systems-resilience problem: highly optimized supply chains can become brittle when the operating environment changes. The article’s core concern is contested logistics: an adversary can disrupt sustainment across multiple domains, rather than allowing U.S. forces to move supplies through uncontested routes. A key caveat is that the article is a strategic warning and prioritization argument, not a public proof that specific Army logistics nodes will fail in a particular scenario.

The U.S. Army has recently discussed “contested logistics” as a doctrinal problem, meaning that an adversary can directly target or disrupt sustainment across land, air, sea, space, cyber, and information domains. This concern grew partly because post-9/11 U.S. operations often relied on relatively uncontested supply lines, while a peer or near-peer war would likely make those lines much harder to protect.
